# Frameworks

Named frameworks the lenses reference. GTM material draws on *Product-Led Growth* by Wes Bush.

---

## Name The Segment

Segments live in different realities — name the one this decision serves, and its reality, before judging it. Don't average across everyone.

For each candidate segment ask: what are they trying to get done, what do they prioritize (speed, control, scale, cost), and how does their context differ from ours? A product that serves "everyone" usually serves the loudest segment by accident.

Illustrative segments (adapt to your product): individual self-serve users who prioritize simplicity and speed; small-business operators focused on growth and efficiency; independent professionals running everything alone; agencies and partners working at scale with repeatable workflows; developers who prioritize flexibility and control.

---

## Tradeoff Axes

Strategy is the tradeoffs you will defend. Force an explicit choice on the axes that apply:

- Depth vs Breadth
- Speed vs Quality
- Guided vs Open
- Self-serve vs Sales-assisted
- Free vs Paid (or gated)
- Simple now vs Scalable later

---

## Product Shape

How users use the product is a decision, not a UX detail. Anything can take any shape — an agent, algorithm, funnel, or dashboard. "AI-first" is not a product strategy on its own; the product still needs an opinion.

- **Guided** — the product walks users step by step.
- **Suggested** — the product proposes the next best move.
- **Open** — the product stays open and lets experts control the outcome.

Freedom and guidance are tools, not opposites. Pick the shape deliberately and defend it.

## Value & GTM Funnel

A product is how users move through value. Find where the product loses them.

discover value → understand value → experience value → trust value → decide to pay

## MOAT (acquisition-model fit)

Pick the acquisition model that fits the market:

- **M — Market strategy:** dominant (best solution, lowest price, needs a very large market), disruptive (simpler/cheaper take on an existing tool), or differentiated (solve a specific job far better, at a premium, often complex)?
- **O — Ocean conditions:** red ocean (existing demand) or blue ocean (creating it)?
- **A — Audience:** bottom-up (product-led) or top-down (sales-led) buyers?
- **T — Time-to-value:** fast "aha" without help → product-led can work.

Rule of thumb: bottom-up + fast time-to-value + red ocean → go product-led. Complex product + top-down buyers + blue ocean → start sales-led.

What fits which market strategy:

- **Dominant** → freemium or free trial (capture share fast). e.g. Shopify, Uber.
- **Disruptive** → freemium thrives (draws prospects off incumbents). e.g. Wave vs legacy accounting.
- **Differentiated** → free trial or demo; freemium rarely works (market-size limits, slow time-to-value).

Acquisition models:

- **Free trial** — full or partial product, free for a limited time.
- **Freemium** — part of the product, free forever, no time limit.
- **Demo** — sales-led evaluation; best for large, complex deals.

## Selling Motion

The motion must match the GTM model; they repel each other when they don't.

- **Bottom-up** (Slack, Figma, DocuSign): land with the end user; spreads organically; demands fast time-to-value and simplicity; low CAC, short cycles; runs on free trial or freemium; pairs with product-led GTM.
- **Top-down** (SAP, Oracle, Salesforce): sell to the decision-maker; org-wide rollouts; high contract value plus services revenue; long cycles, high CAC, revenue concentrated in a few accounts; pairs with sales-led GTM.

## Signal Taxonomy

Classify what a signal is before acting on it. The type changes how much it should move the decision. Read behavior over stated opinion.

- **Demand signal** — repeated requests for the same outcome. Strong when volume + recency are high.
- **Workaround evidence** — users hack around a limitation ("I export to a spreadsheet and…"). Often the strongest signal: pain severe enough to invest effort.
- **Friction / drop-off** — abandons, retries, rage paths, support volume on one step. Points to where value is lost, not what to add.
- **Switching intent** — "how do I export," "is there an alternative," cancellation reasons. An exit signal; treat as urgent trust/value failure.
- **Feature request** — a named solution. Interpret the need behind it; do not build it literally (faster-horse risk).
- **Competitive-gap mention** — "competitor X does Y." Validate against the competitor landscape, don't copy reflexively.

For each: what is the underlying need, how strong (volume × recency × effort), and what would confirm it?

## Competitor Landscape

Map across four types, not just direct rivals, before judging whether a tradeoff is defensible:

- **Direct** — same user, same problem.
- **Indirect** — same problem, different approach.
- **Alternatives / substitutes** — manual workflows, agencies, spreadsheets, "do nothing."
- **Adjacent** — integrations or platforms that could absorb the job.

"We have no competition" is always wrong — the **status quo** (spreadsheets, email, an agency, doing nothing) is the default competitor, and usually the real one. Map it first.

Use it to separate **table stakes** (everyone has it — gating or skipping it is a liability) from **differentiator** (where your defended tradeoff actually wins). For the differentiator, name the **moat type** that defends it: network effects, switching costs, scale economics, brand, regulatory/IP, distribution, or data advantage. A differentiator with no moat type is a feature competitors copy next quarter.

## Value-Metric Pricing

Price around a value metric — how you measure the value exchanged — not a feature list. It ties what customers pay to the value they receive, so revenue grows as they succeed (Slack → active users, Claude → tokens used, PayPal → revenue processed).

A good value metric passes three tests:

1. **Easy to understand** — customers instantly grasp what they pay for.
2. **Aligns with value received** — more usage means more value and more cost.
3. **Grows with the customer** — spend expands as the account scales.

Value-metric pricing churns far less than feature-tier pricing (ProfitWell: up to ~75% less; more for outcome-based metrics). Don't default to per-seat if seats don't reflect value.
